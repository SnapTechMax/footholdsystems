## Part 2 — The structures

Six shapes account for nearly all of it.

**1. The qualifier open.** First line names exactly who this is for, in a way
that repels everyone else. "If your business buys ads…" / "This is not for
beginners." The audience is extracted, not attracted.

**2. Skip-the-fluff.** Refuse the runway. State the claim flat, add "period,"
then promise the explanation. "So let's skip the fluff. X does Y, period.
Here's how."

**3. The bet.** Put something at risk in the headline. "Your ads scale or it's
free." A claim with a consequence attached reads as true in a way an adjective
never does.

**4. The specific-loss calculation.** Do the arithmetic on their money in front
of them. "$100,000 on ads this year, and $15,000 of that is going to be wasted."
Never a vague "you're losing money."

**5. The named mechanism.** Not "our service" but a thing with a name: the
tracking AI, the script, the challenge. A named mechanism can be believed in.

**6. Curiosity CTA.** The click buys an explanation, not a purchase. "If you
click this ad, I'll explain in extreme detail how it all works."

## Part 3 — The rules, applied

1. **Benefit lands before the "see more" fold.** Mobile truncates around 125
   characters. Everything load-bearing goes first.
2. **Curiosity beats explanation.** Give them a reason to need the rest.
3. **Never sell in the ad.** The ad buys a click. The page sells.
4. **Repel deliberately.** Specificity that excludes is what makes the right
   reader lean in.
5. **One focal point per image.** One subject, high contrast, no clutter.
6. **Do not look like an ad.** Native, plain, slightly unpolished beats polished.
7. **Headlines create a question or a challenge**, never a suggestion.
8. **Message over production.** A plain image with the right sentence beats a
   beautiful one with the wrong sentence.
9. **Numbers must be real.** His are real and audited. That is the entire reason
   they work.

## Part 3b — What makes it a scroll-stopper

The rules above are about copy. This one is about the largest text on the
image, which is a different job with a different bar.

Body copy is read by somebody who has already stopped. The big type is what
makes them stop. A line that closes a paragraph beautifully is a **conclusion**,
and a conclusion is something you nod at, not something that arrests a thumb.
"Vague businesses are unrecommendable" is a fine hammer and a dead image.

Look at what Becker actually puts in front of people:

> "If your business buys ads, I can guarantee that your tracking is off by
> 30-40%."
> "You're going to spend $100,000 on ads this year, and $15,000 of that is going
> to be wasted."
> "This is not for beginners."

None of them is a summary of the argument. Every one is an accusation or an
event, pointed at one reader, leaving a question the click has to answer.

Three things fall out of that, and the generator enforces all three:

1. **Under a second to read.** Eight words, forty-eight characters. Longer than
   that is a caption being read by somebody who already stopped.
2. **Somebody is in it.** A "you" or a "we", a real number, an overheard line in
   quotes, or a question put to the reader. Without one it is a statement about
   the category rather than about them.
3. **It turns.** Setup then reversal, or a direct question. One flat declarative
   is a label, and a label is the thing that got scrolled past.

4. **It names the actor.** Somebody scrolling a feed was not thinking about AI
   ten seconds ago. "Ask it right now. Whose name comes back?" reads to them as
   a riddle: the first thing they do is wonder what "it" is, and by then they
   have scrolled. It has to say ChatGPT, or AI, in the big type. Naming only
   Google does not count, because an ad that names Google and nothing else
   reads as an SEO ad, which is the confusion this product exists to correct.

4b. **It names the other party too.** The same failure one step over. "ChatGPT
   named them" leaves the reader asking who "them" is, and the answer is the
   entire emotional charge of the ad: the business down the road that does what
   they do. Say **your competitor**. A pronoun throws away the only word in the
   sentence the reader has feelings about.

And a fifth, which is about the ad rather than the line:

5. **It does not restate the copy underneath it.** If the image says what the
   headline says, the ad says one thing twice and stops nobody. Checked on
   shared content words, not just shared phrasing, because "Your homepage fits
   everyone, so it fits nobody" and "It matches everyone, so it matches nobody"
   are the same sentence wearing different clothes.

Applied to the big type on the statement and comparison concepts. The answer
panel's largest text is a real question a buyer typed, which is concrete by
construction.

### The verdict line

The answer panel's caption is not support type, it is the ruling on what the
panel above just showed, and it has its own rule.

> **Reports:** "You rank first on Google for this. It did not matter here."
> **Rules:** "You rank first on Google for this. That doesn't matter anymore."

Same fact. The first one is the outcome of one search: true, small, arguable,
and the reader can tell themselves it was a fluke. The second says what is now
the case, which is the claim the entire page is built on. Only one of them
sounds like somebody who knows.

Two mechanical tells separate them, and both are checked:

- **It does not scope itself to the instance.** No "here", no "this time", no
  "in this case". Those words shrink a rule back down to an anecdote.
- **It does not report in the past what it can assert in the present.** No
  "did", "was", "were", "had", "happened", "used to".

Not a tense parser, and a line can still be limp while passing. The rule
narrows the failure mode; the writing does the rest.

### The copy carries the same rules

The image rules stopped hooks making the reader supply the subject. The copy
had the same problem and one place where it is worse: in a Facebook feed the
primary text sits **above** the image, so its first line is read before the
reader has seen anything at all. "You will never know it happened" was the
first thing a stranger read, and "it" pointed at something nobody had mentioned
yet.

Prose is not image text, though, and a blanket ban would be wrong. Once AI has
been named, "it" is ordinary good writing, and repeating "ChatGPT" in every
sentence reads like a machine. So the rule follows the reading order:

| Where | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Fold line** | Name the actor outright | First thing read, nothing precedes it |
| **Primary text** | Name it before you lean on it | A pronoun is fine once its antecedent exists |
| **Headline, description** | No bare pronoun at all | Right column and inbox show these with no primary text above |

Sixty-one lines were rewritten against it. Every qualifier and every hook now
names AI or ChatGPT, because either one can end up as the fold depending on
which structure the set draws.

> **Was:** "The crawler arrived, found nothing it could quote, and left."
> **Now:** "The AI crawler arrived, found nothing it could quote, and left."

> **Was:** "It is not rewarding the best operator. It is rewarding the best-documented one."
> **Now:** "AI is not rewarding the best operator. It is rewarding the best-documented one."

Note what the second one keeps. The sentence still says "It" the second time,
because by then the reader has been told. That is the whole rule: name it once,
then write normally.

**Where this is deliberately not enforced.** Across paragraphs, "they" almost
always points at the noun in the sentence before, and a rule that cannot parse
that flags correct writing more often than it catches anything. "Both positions
were defensible right up until they were not" is fine. The third-party check
runs on the cold and short fields, where a bare pronoun genuinely has nothing
to point at anywhere in the ad.

### The panel never names a business

The answer in the panel says **(YOUR COMPETITOR)**, in the accent colour. Not a
real business, and not an invented one either.

A real name puts somebody else's brand in your ad. An invented one is a
fabricated record dressed as a screenshot, which `offer.md` rules out for the
same reason it rules out invented testimonials.

But the placeholder is also the better ad, which is the actual argument for it:

> "Clearwater Glass lists same-day service and coverage areas directly."
> "(YOUR COMPETITOR) lists same-day service and coverage areas directly."

The first asks the reader to accept a stranger's name and then map it onto
somebody they know. The second makes them do the naming, and the name they
supply is a business they have lost work to. You cannot write a name more
threatening than the one already in their head, so stop trying and leave the
slot open.

Place names stay as they are. "San Dimas" is where the reader lives, not a claim.

---

Deliberately not applied to the hammers in the copy bank. "You did not lose.
You were never entered." comes straight out of voice.md and is doing something
else: it is the turn at the end of an argument the reader has already been
walked through, not a verdict delivered cold.

| Fails | Why | Passes |
|---|---|---|
| UNRECOMMENDABLE. | No stake, no turn, no actor | "QUALITY WORK." AI CAN'T USE THAT. |
| SILENCE READS AS UNPROVEN. | A conclusion, and nobody in it | CHATGPT LOOKED YOU UP. FOUND SILENCE. |
| PAGES vs BUSINESSES | A label, not a sentence | YOU RANK FIRST ON GOOGLE. CHATGPT NAMED THEM. |
| ASK IT. WHOSE NAME COMES BACK? | "It" is a riddle to cold traffic | ASK CHATGPT. WHOSE NAME COMES BACK? |
| YOU'RE BETTER. IT PICKED THEM. | Same, one word from working | YOU'RE BETTER. CHATGPT PICKED THEM. |

## Part 4 — Where FootHold must diverge, and why

Becker's spine is the guarantee: *your ads scale or you don't pay.* FootHold
cannot run that play, and must not fake it.

- `offer.md`: **no guaranteed ranking or recommendation, ever.** The sales page
  says outright that anyone promising one is lying. An ad that implies otherwise
  contradicts the page it points at.
- `offer.md`: **no invented statistics.** Becker's "52%" is measured. FootHold
  has no equivalent figure and must not manufacture one.
- The $15,000 / 180-day guarantee belongs to **tier 3 only**, which is a closed
  funnel sold to existing tier-2 customers. It must never appear in cold traffic.

The honest substitution, and it is a stronger hook rather than a weaker one:

> **Becker's move:** "We guarantee it, or you don't pay."
> **FootHold's move:** "Nobody can guarantee this. Anyone who says they can is
> lying to you. Here is what can actually be measured."

Refusing the guarantee out loud, in a category full of people offering one, is
the same contrarian shape doing the same job. It creates the question, splits
the audience, and is true.
