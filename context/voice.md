# FootHold — Voice

*How the business writes, and how it must never sound*

> [!info] How to read this
> **Known** = observed in the code or stated directly by Max.
> **Assumed** = a hypothesis that reached live copy without customer research.
> `[UNSURE]` = genuinely unknown, left blank rather than guessed at.

### How the business talks

Two registers, deliberately different, because they do different jobs.

**Sales page — Alex Becker / Hyros direct response.** Short punchy paragraphs, a
bold one-line hammer after a block of setup, a named mechanism (the four
signals), an explicit us-versus-them comparison, blunt objection handling
including admitting what cannot be guaranteed, and a qualification section that
tells some readers not to buy.

**Email sequence — André Chaperon.** One serialised story told in order, not 22
self-contained tips. Open loops: 19 of 22 emails end on something they do not
close. Nothing to click for the first 19 emails. The offer arrives at email 20,
once, as a text link. Written to one person reading on a phone between jobs.

**Reports** — plain, specific, unpatronising. Written for the owner, never in
the scanner's vocabulary.

### How it should never sound

- Hyped. No exclamation marks anywhere in the sequence, and the one that exists
  is inside quoted copy being mocked.
- Like a newsletter. The email shell strips the coloured canvas, the masthead,
  the horizontal rule and the padded button, because a button exists in exactly
  one kind of email.
- Like an agency. No "strategy retainers", no ninety-page audits.
- Certain about things nobody can be certain about.
- Like a chatbot. See the humanizer notes below.

### Phrases it uses

- "Named or not named."
- "You did not lose. You were never entered."
- "Vague businesses are not disliked. They are unrecommendable."
- "Level is a good place to be. It is not the same as being the one that gets named."
- "It is not rewarding the best operator. It is rewarding the best-documented one."
- "Silence does not read as neutral. It reads as unproven."
- "There is one answer."

### Phrases it avoids

- Em dashes. Reduced from 72 to 5 across all customer-facing copy; the five
  survivors are price separators inside buttons and one plain-text divider.
- British spelling. US throughout: optimize, not optimise.
- "Not just X, it's Y" and its relatives. Cut from 9 to 5 because the shape
  became audible.
- Overused AI vocabulary: delve, crucial, pivotal, tapestry, testament,
  showcase, vibrant, robust, seamless, leverage.
- Sales-brochure language: nestled, boasts, breathtaking, must-visit.
- Anything the humanizer flags as an inflated-importance construction.

### Examples of good writing

**Sales page, the hammer:**
> If that name is your competitor, you did not come second.
> You were not in the conversation at all.

**Sales page, honest objection handling:**
> No. Nobody can, and anyone who tells you otherwise is either lying to you or
> does not understand what they are selling.

**Email 1, the story open:**
> Then, because it seemed like the honest thing to do before pointing it at
> anyone else, I ran it on footholdsystems.com. Twenty out of a hundred. Grade F.

**Email 17, admitting an error:**
> A friend ran his site through it. The report told him his business name did
> not bring up his website. It was not true.

**Report, the cold-search caveat:**
> How this was measured: a cold search. No login, no history, no location, no
> personalisation. Search your own name in your own browser and you will very
> likely see yourself first. Both results are real. This is the one an AI sees.

### House rules

Short sentences. Plain English. No hype. No exclamation marks. No em dashes. US
spelling. Anything technical must be copy-pasteable or it does not belong.
Assume a busy owner reading on a phone between jobs.
